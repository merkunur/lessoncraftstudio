## 1. The pitch
"Each number is a drop of coloured beads — drag any two together and they melt into one drop; two that make exactly ten fuse into a shining pearl, and whatever order you join them, the last drop is always the same size."

## 2. Why a child will want it again (the fun)
- **Drops are irresistible.** Three round, glassy drops of beads wobble gently on the bench (a 2 % breathing scale, 3 s cycle, out of phase). Dragging one onto another makes them melt together with a squash-and-stretch wobble and a soft "bloop" — like joining two water drops on a window.
- **The pearl.** When a merge holds exactly ten beads, the beads snap into a perfect 2 × 5 ten-frame inside the drop, the drop's rim turns gold and shines (a 300 ms light sweep), and it gives a clear glass "ting". Children hunt for the pearl.
- **The surprise that never changes.** After the round, "Try another way" replays the round's other two orders as ghost drops side by side: three different journeys, and all three end in exactly the same drop. A child who sees that once never forgets that order does not matter in adding.
- **Free play ("Drop lab"):** pull single beads OUT of a drop with a finger to make new drops, merge anything, chase pearls; nothing is graded.

## 3. The skill and the misconception
- **Skill (1.OA.A.2):** solve problems that call for adding three whole numbers whose sum is ≤ 20 — using the properties of operations: add in any order, and look for a pair that makes ten (or a double) first.
- **Misconception 1 — adding only two numbers** (the third is forgotten): typing 7 for 3 + 4 + 5. **Push back:** on the miss the apparatus merges exactly the two drops whose total the child typed — 3 and 4 melt into a 7-drop — and the 5-drop is left alone on the bench, pulsing: your number is these two; this one is still outside.
- **Misconception 2 — order changes the answer / must go left to right:** **Push back:** any two drops merge; the bond does not care which; "Try another way" shows the three orders end in the same drop.
- **Misconception 3 — counting all from 1:** **Push back:** merged drops always repack their beads into ten-frame rows, and a full ten becomes a pearl, so 10 + 6 can be READ (a pearl and six) instead of counted bead by bead; the drop with the pearl visibly makes the last step easy.

## 5. The hand
- **Drag a drop onto another drop** (or tap one, then the other): the dragged drop follows the finger within 30 ms, wobbling; when it overlaps the target by ≥ 30 % the target bulges toward it; on release they melt (350 ms: squash 0.9/1.1 → round, a "bloop" tone whose pitch drops as the drop grows), the beads repack into ten-frame rows (each bead travels to its new place in 250 ms). The merged drop moves into the part circle of the TARGET; the emptied part circle shows a faint dashed ring.
- **Exactly ten** after a merge: the ten beads click into the 2 × 5 frame, the rim turns gold with a light sweep, a glass "ting".
- **More than ten:** the pearl forms from the first ten (the ten-frame in a gold ring) and the rest gather in a droplet stuck to its side.
- **The last merge:** when only one drop remains it rises along the bond lines into the whole circle (400 ms) and sits behind the answer box.
- **Undo:** the "↶" chip under the bond splits the last merge back into its two drops (they pop apart with a small spring). Keyboard: U.
- **Keypad:** digits into the answer box (two digits max); backspace removes.
- **Free play "Drop lab":** an extra chip "＋ drop" adds a new single-bead drop; dragging a bead out of a drop pulls it free as a new 1-drop; merging is as above; no Check.

**Order:** order-only reshuffle per pass; never the same order twice.

## 7. Check, the misses, the success
- **Check with an empty answer box:** no miss; the keypad glows; bubble: **"Join the drops, or add in your head — then type how many beads."**
- **Miss 1:** *if the typed number equals the sum of two of the drops* (the forgotten-third error): those two drops merge by themselves (the apparatus does what the child did), and the left-out drop pulses with a ring. Bubble: **"Your {n} is these two drops. One drop is still outside!"** *Otherwise:* all unmerged drops merge in the best order (the ten pair first, else the double, else A + B), slowly, and stop with the pearl showing; bubble: **"Look for the pearl. A full ten and how many more?"**
- **Miss 2:** the final drop's beads get small numerals: the ten inside the pearl shows one big "10" on its ring, and the extra beads are numbered 11, 12, … in reading order. Bubble: **"Ten in the pearl — count on from 10."**
- **A new move or keypress takes the help away** until the next Check (merged drops stay merged; the child can undo).
- **Success:** the answer settles into the whole circle with a gold ring pulse; the final drop gives one happy bounce; the bond lines glow in turn from each part to the whole. The bubble writes the child's own path as equations, e.g. **"7 + 3 = 10 and 10 + 4 = 14"** (if they merged 7 and 3 first) or the plain **"3 + 4 + 5 = 12"** if they typed without merging. A small chip **"Try another way"** plays the other orders as three ghost columns ending in the same drop: **"Any order — still {total}!"**

## 13. The four questions
1. **Would a 5–8-year-old choose it again?** Yes — melting drops together is pure toy pleasure, and finding the golden pearl is a little treasure hunt every round.
2. **Would a teacher put it on the board?** Yes — "which two would you join first?" is the exact 1.OA.A.2 discussion; the pearl makes "make a ten first" visible, and the any-order replay proves the commutative and associative properties without a word.
3. **Would a parent trust it?** Calm, wordless apparatus; the child types the total; the help shows structure (a ten and more), never just the answer.
4. **The invention:** numbers are drops that melt together in any order, two that make ten fuse into a pearl, and every order ends in the same drop.
